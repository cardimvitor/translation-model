Label normalizer: what we built and how the models compare

Product names, item labels and addresses in 16 languages turned into English, offline, on a phone. From the first version (Nov 4) to the two final models, v9 and v8 Fast (Nov 14).

{{KPIS}}
How to read the scores. Exact is the share of outputs that match a reference English name word for word (after lowercasing). chrF (0–100) is a character-overlap score that gives partial credit, e.g. "cotton t-shirt" vs "cotton tee shirt". Two test sets, never used in training: Labels = 320 product labels I wrote by hand in 16 languages; Real names = 993 real marketplace product names from the Amazon Berkeley Objects set, held out before training. Accurate is the full system (Fast rules, then the small neural model when Fast is unsure); model alone is the neural model without Fast.

The two final models

v9 is the model to use: v8 Complete (pretrained base, real product names, a shallow decoder for speed, one expert per language) taught 200k more short labels with the post-training tool. v8 Fast is the smaller, faster cut for quick validation. Both use only teachers and data that allow commercial use.

Earlier: v6 and v7

What changed. Reviewing everything showed one weak point: the neural part was trained from nothing on 4 CPU cores, and alone it scored about 32 on the labels while the big translators score 76–95. v6 does not start from nothing. It takes a compact pretrained translator (OPUS-MT mul-en, 77M parameters, CC BY 4.0), trims its vocabulary to our 16 languages (adding the Korean pieces it lacked), and fine-tunes it for 8.5 hours on everything we had built: the v5 MAX data plus 58k new short-label pairs made from Amazon search queries. After 30 minutes it already beat every earlier model.

3. What we did, in order

  • v1–v2 (Nov 4–5). Fast: place-name gazetteer (GeoNames), transliteration for 9 scripts, a product word list, an English check. Runs in ~2 ms per label, in Python and in the browser with identical output.
  • v3 (Nov 5–6). First neural model (7M parameters, NLLB teacher, 100 min). Weight study: removing one decoder attention head helped, so it was kept.
  • v4 (Nov 6–8). Three models: A (NLLB), B (OPUS-MT + M2M100, commercial use OK), C (both). 81k product titles, back-translation into 16 languages, restart-safe training, checkpoint averaging, weight edits per model.
  • v5 (Nov 8–10). Teachers' own English answers added as training targets (knowledge distillation, filtered), Tencent Hy-MT2 added as a third teacher, models B2, C2, H, ALL3, HY. Then MAX: no size limit on data, 35k real multilingual product names (ABO), 60k more titles, 10 h training.
  • Products and word combinations (Nov 10). 952 new product words and 2–3 word phrases mined from the Amazon ESCI and ABO catalogues, translated and round-trip checked, added to every model's word list (1,841 automatic terms in total).
  • New decision rule (Nov 10). When to trust the neural model instead of Fast, tuned on held-out training data. Biggest single gain of v5.
  • v6 (Nov 11–12). Review of the whole project, then the final model: a pretrained compact translator fine-tuned on all our data, new short-label data, a SentencePiece tokenizer written for the phone runtime, better 8-bit conversion, a new decision rule.
  • JEPA (Nov 10–11). A self-supervised encoder pretrained to predict the meaning of a label from another language, used two ways, compared against a baseline trained the same total time.

4. Changing the weights after training

After each model finished, I tested switching off each attention head and each feed-forward block one at a time on one held-out slice, kept a change only if it also helped on a second, separate slice, then tried a 15-minute retraining with the change in place. The final call needs the held-out score up and the hand-written labels not down. Checkpoint averaging (mean of the last 3 saved weights) was tested on every model too.

{{EDITS}}

Scores are model-alone chrF: held-out slice / hand-written labels. "Changes tested" counts single head or block switch-offs that looked useful in the first pass.

What this showed: switching off one or two heads gives small, real gains on about half the models (they had a head that hurt). Short retraining helps when the model is undertrained (MAX, ALL3) and hurts when it is already settled (C2). Bigger cuts (whole layers, more heads) always hurt.

5. Deciding when to use the neural model

Old rule: use the neural model only for the words Fast could not translate. New rule: if Fast understood the label (at least one known word) or it looks like an address, keep Fast; otherwise let the neural model translate the whole label, and keep its answer if it is English and keeps the words Fast was sure of. Tuned on the held-out training split, not on the test sets.

{{RULE}}

6. JEPA

What it is. A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture learns by predicting the meaning vector of hidden or translated text instead of the text itself. Here: an encoder reads a label in one language; a predictor must output the vector that a slowly-updated copy of the encoder (EMA target) gives for the English name, and for masked-out spans of the label. No characters are generated, so it learns meaning, not spelling.

Making it work. The first run collapsed: every label got the same vector. Fixed with centering of the target vectors and a variance/covariance penalty (VICReg). Measured by retrieval: among 1,000 held-out pairs, how often the English name nearest to a foreign label's vector is the right one (chance = 0.1%).

7. What didn't work

  • Word pieces (BPE) instead of characters. Shorter sequences, but on 4 CPU cores the models stayed stuck repeating common words after hours. Characters learn much faster at this size.
  • A 52M-parameter MAX. About 3× slower per step; in the time available it would have seen too little data. The 22M design wins on CPU.
  • Picking one teacher per language. The held-out data was written by OPUS-MT and NLLB, which biases the choice toward them. Using every teacher's filtered answers works better.
  • Hy-MT2 as main data source (HY). Best teacher, but at ~2.5 s per title only 8k pairs were affordable, and its answers in the mixed models (H, ALL3) did not beat OPUS-MT alone.
  • Large weight cuts and long post-edit retraining. See section 4.
  • Training small models from nothing. The biggest lesson of the review: ten hours of from-scratch CPU training reached 38 on held-out titles; fine-tuning a pretrained model passed that in under 15 minutes and ended at 64.
  • Default 8-bit conversion and 7-bit weights for v6. Both lost too much accuracy; see the v6 section.
