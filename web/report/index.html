Label Normalizer

An offline system that turns product names, item labels and addresses written in 16 languages into English, on a phone or a server. This page covers everything from the first version to v14: what was built, how each model was made, what was measured, what failed, and where every file is.

The result

Two models are final. v14 is the one to use: the most accurate, 57 MB, about 6 ms per short label. v8 Fast is a smaller, faster cut (42 MB, about 3 ms) meant for quick validation runs. Both were trained only with teachers and data that allow commercial use, with attribution.

The task is narrow on purpose. Inputs are short: a product label ("кроссовки для бега"), a marketplace product name, an address, a city. The output is the English a catalogue would use ("running shoes"), or the romanized form when transliteration is asked for.

How to read the scores. Exact is the share of outputs equal to an accepted English answer after lowercasing. chrF (0–100) gives partial credit by character overlap, so "cotton tee shirt" for "cotton t-shirt" scores high but not 100. The two main test sets were never used for training: 320 product labels written by hand in 16 languages, and 993 real marketplace product names held out before training. Later tests (mixed-language labels, places) were each written before the work they measure.

How the system works

A label goes through four steps. The first three are rules and take well under a millisecond; only the fourth runs the neural model.

  1. English check. A 100 KB character model says whether the label is already English. If it is clearly English it is returned as it is. Since v12 each word is also checked against a list of 26,000 English words, so "led ampul" is not mistaken for English.
  2. Places and addresses. The place list (2.1 million names of countries, regions, cities, towns, villages, islands and districts) gives the English name. Address rules translate the street-type word and romanize the street name: "ул. Ленина 5" becomes "Lenina Street 5".
  3. Known words. A product word list (about 1,800 terms per language) translates labels in which every word is known.
  4. Neural translator. Everything else goes to a 57 MB translation model with a small expert for each language. Its answer is used when its confidence is high enough; otherwise the romanized form is kept.

Every answer carries a reliable or needs review mark. When transliteration is asked for instead of translation, only romanization rules run, with tables for Korean, Chinese and Japanese personal names and separate rules for Ukrainian, Greek, Arabic and Hindi.

The same logic exists twice, in Python and in JavaScript, and the two are compared on 3,018 inputs after every change. They give identical answers on all rule steps; the neural step differs on fewer than 1% of labels, where two candidate answers are nearly tied.

The reliable mark

An answer is marked reliable when it is a known place, clearly English text, made only of known words, or a neural answer with confidence of at least 0.82. The threshold was set on separate tuning labels.

How it was made, in order

Rules first (Nov 4–5)

The English check and Fast were built before any neural model: a place list from GeoNames and Unicode CLDR, romanization rules for nine writing systems, address rules, and a hand-written product word list. Python and JavaScript versions were kept identical from the first day. Fast alone scores 42.6 chrF on labels and handles addresses in about 0.3 ms.

Small models from nothing: v3, v4, v5 (Nov 5–10)

Twelve character-level models of 7 to 22 million parameters were trained from random weights on product titles translated into the 16 languages by larger models (the teachers). More data, longer training, a second and third teacher, and the teachers' own answers as extra targets moved label chrF from 43 to 51. Adding 35,000 real product names written by people (v5 MAX) lifted real-name chrF by 7 points, the largest single gain of this phase.

JEPA experiment (Nov 10–11)

A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture was built and tuned: the encoder learns by predicting the meaning vector of a label, then a translator is trained on top. It worked technically (the right English name out of 1,000 was found 19% of the time by vector alone) but a baseline given the same hours of ordinary training scored higher (45.9 against 43.5).

The pretrained start: v6 (Nov 11–12)

A review showed the weak point: the neural part scored about 32 chrF alone, while large translators score 76 to 95. v6 starts from a pretrained translator (OPUS-MT mul-en, 77M parameters, 34 chrF untrained on this task), with its vocabulary cut to our languages and 3,091 missing pieces added, mostly Korean. Fine-tuning for 8.5 hours on all product data plus 58,000 short labels made from real search queries gave 79.6 on labels and 66.9 on real names. The 8-bit conversion needed per-channel quantization to keep that accuracy.

Experts per language: v7 (Nov 12)

v6 was frozen and a small pair of matrices per language was added to every layer, routed by the language detector, so only the detected language's parameters are active. Gain: 1.2 chrF.

Smaller and faster: v8 (Nov 13)

Profiling showed most time went to the decoder and the output layer. v8 keeps v6's 6-layer encoder but only 2 decoder layers, an output layer with English pieces only, and experts in the encoder only. Result: half the size, twice the speed, and 3.5 chrF better on labels. v8 Fast is cut further from it.

A tool for adding data: v9 and v10 (Nov 13–14)

The post-training tool takes a parent model and new pairs, trains with the old data replayed, exports, retunes the rules and prints PASS or FAIL against the parent. v9 used it with 200,000 more short labels; v10 added beam search and common terms written in all 16 languages. Labels reached 86.8 chrF.

Cleaning the data with a judge: v11 (Nov 14)

v10 was run over 28,493 training labels and missed 10,685. Hy-MT2-7B re-translated 8,331 of them. In about seven of ten cases the training answer itself was wrong or poor, not the model. 5,976 bad pairs were removed and 5,921 corrected ones taught. v11 also added the English double check (9 of 320 test labels had been returned untranslated) and the reliable mark.

One foreign word: v12 (Nov 15)

A new English + Turkish test showed labels like "led ampul" coming back untranslated. Training on 44,000 mixed-language pairs made ordinary labels worse and was rejected, as were blends of old and new weights. The neural model alone already scored 90.6 on the test; the loss was in the rules. A word-level English check fixed it without touching the weights.

Places of the whole world: v13 and v14 (Nov 15)

The place list was rebuilt twice from GeoNames: first every town of 500 people or more with regions and preferred English names, then villages from 200 people, islands, districts, neighbourhoods and every place with a local-script name (2,147,613 names, 77 MB). Address rules were extended (postal codes, German street names, abbreviations across languages, Korean and Japanese forms), personal-name tables and Ukrainian and Greek rules were added, and about 7,500 Arabic word readings were mined from place names because Arabic is written without short vowels. The two places tests went from 109 to 153 of 160 and from 75 to 91 of 101.

Data

TechniqueWhat it does here
Back-translationEnglish product titles and search queries translated into each language by a teacher model, kept only when they translate back to the original.
Real paired data35,000 marketplace names written by people in 12 languages with an English name.
Short-label dataSearch queries as stand-ins for labels, since the tests are short and titles are long.
Teacher as judgeA stronger model re-translates what the student gets wrong, separating model errors from bad training answers.
Held-out disciplineQueries equal to a test answer are removed from training; each new test is written before the work it measures.

Model

TechniqueWhat it does here
Pretrained startFine-tuning a 77M-parameter translator instead of training from nothing.
Vocabulary surgeryUnused word pieces removed, missing ones added, separate source and English-only output vocabularies.
Shallow decoder6 encoder layers, 2 decoder layers: the encoder runs once, the decoder once per output piece.
Language expertsA 1.6 MB adapter per language in the encoder, selected by the language detector.
Post-training with replayNew pairs sampled six times more often while all old data is replayed, with an automatic pass/fail gate.

Head switch-off testsEach attention head disabled in turn on the small models; a change kept only if two held-out sets agree.

What we learned

  • The starting point mattered more than everything else combined. Seven days and twelve from-scratch models moved label chrF from 43 to 51. One 8.5-hour fine-tune of a pretrained translator moved it to 80.
  • Data that looks like the task beats more data. Real product names and short search queries each gave larger gains than doubling the translated titles.
  • Much of the remaining error was in the training data. Seven of ten labels the model "missed" had a wrong or poor training answer.
  • Measure the model alone before retraining. For mixed-language labels, more training hurt; the fault was a rule that never let the label reach the model.
  • A smaller model can be better and faster when the cut follows a measurement: most decoder layers and the multilingual output layer were not earning their cost.
  • Rules stay necessary. Places and addresses are where a small neural model is weakest and a list is near-perfect.
  • Write the test before the fix. Every regression in the last four versions was caught by a test written beforehand or by the automatic gate, not by reading outputs.
  • A gate that can say FAIL is worth more than a model that looks good. It rejected two training runs that would otherwise have shipped.

Limits

  • Not better than Hy-MT2-7B on short labels (87.7 against 95.1 chrF) or on mixed English + Turkish labels (89.7 against 93.9). Closing that gap needs far more of its answers as training data than a CPU can produce.
  • Not error-free. About one answer in four on short labels differs from the reference; many are near-synonyms, some are wrong. The reliable mark tells which to check.
  • No list of streets. Street-type words are translated in many forms and languages, but street names themselves are romanized. A list of every street would be hundreds of gigabytes.
  • Places still missed: 17 of the 261 items in the two places tests, mainly some Japanese readings, Thai address words written without spaces, places named in a third language, and Ukrainian text with no Ukrainian-only letters.
  • The place list is large: 77 MB, and about 875 MB of memory in Python with every writing system loaded (the v13 list: 32 MB and about 480 MB). A phone loads only the writing systems it meets.
  • Korean and Thai remain the weakest languages for product labels.
  • Test sets are small (320 labels from 20 products, 64 mixed labels, 261 place items). Differences under about 1 chrF between neighbouring versions are within noise.
  • Automated checks on GitHub have never run for this pull request: every run ends in a few seconds with no runner assigned, which is an account setting. All checks were run on the build machine.
