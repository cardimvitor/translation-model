How to read the scores. Exact is the share of outputs that match a reference English name word for word (after lowercasing). chrF (0–100) is a character-overlap score that gives partial credit, e.g. "cotton t-shirt" vs "cotton tee shirt". Two test sets, never used in training: Labels = 320 product labels I wrote by hand in 16 languages; Real names = 993 real marketplace product names from the Amazon Berkeley Objects set, held out before training. Accurate is the full system (Fast rules, then the small neural model when Fast is unsure); model alone is the neural model without Fast.

The two final models

v11 is the model to use: v8 Complete (pretrained base, real product names, a shallow decoder for speed, one expert per language) taught more three times with the post-training tool, decoding with a small beam search, trained on data checked by the 7B model, and marking each answer reliable or needs review. v8 Fast is the smaller, faster cut for quick validation. Both use only teachers and data that allow commercial use.

Hy-MT2-7B is beaten on real product names (it learned the catalogue style from real pairs) but not on short labels, where it scores 95 against 88: a 7-billion-parameter model simply knows more rare product words in every language. v11 used the 7B as a judge and teacher for about 10,000 labels (all a CPU allows in a few hours); closing the rest of the gap needs far more of its answers, which takes a GPU.

v11: cleaning the data with the 7B model as judge

v10 was run over 28,493 short training labels; it missed 10,685. Hy-MT2-7B (too slow to ship, but the most accurate model we have) re-translated 8,331 of them. What it found:

Verdict of the 7BLabelsWhat v11 does with them

So about seven in ten of the labels v10 seemed to get wrong were cases where its training answer was itself wrong or poor. v11 also learned 1,558 real Spanish and Japanese search queries typed by people (translated by the 7B) and 9,600 mixed-language labels.

Mixed-language labels and the English double check

Labels that mix English with another language ("black deri ceket") were never trained before v11. A first attempt joined two labels end to end and made word order worse; it was thrown away. The kept method swaps one known word for its English form inside a real pair, so the answer keeps natural order. Separately, the English check sometimes called a foreign label English (French "veste d'hiver", German "Laptop-Rucksack") and returned it untranslated. v11 asks the neural model when the English check is unsure, and takes its answer only if it is confident and clearly different text. Tuned on data outside the tests: of 2,000 real English queries none was changed, and 87 of 360 wrongly-English foreign labels were caught.

What post-training does on its own, in order: (1) starts from the parent model's weights; (2) fine-tunes on the new pairs mixed with all the old training data, so nothing is forgotten; (3) exports the 8-bit model; (4) retunes the rule that decides when the neural answer is used; (5) runs the tuning labels, test labels, real names and address cases on parent and child; (6) prints PASS or FAIL. If the machine restarts, the same command continues where it stopped. v9, v10 and v11 were all made this way, so the tool has been used for real.

Limits: the vocabulary is fixed, so text in a script the model has never seen cannot be taught this way (the first output line reports how much of the new data is unreadable); a whole new language also needs rules and detector support.

Earlier: v6 and v7

What changed. Reviewing everything showed one weak point: the neural part was trained from nothing on 4 CPU cores, and alone it scored about 32 on the labels while the big translators score 76–95. v6 does not start from nothing. It takes a compact pretrained translator (OPUS-MT mul-en, 77M parameters, CC BY 4.0), trims its vocabulary to our 16 languages (adding the Korean pieces it lacked), and fine-tunes it for 8.5 hours on everything we had built: the v5 MAX data plus 58k new short-label pairs made from Amazon search queries. After 30 minutes it already beat every earlier model.

The default 8-bit conversion cost about 5 chrF on short labels. Scaling each output channel separately recovers most of it at the same size and speed. A 7-bit range (which would make the browser and server round identically) lost too much, so the 8-bit version stays and the two runtimes differ on a few low-confidence answers. Averaging the last three checkpoints was also tested and made no difference on the tuning data, so the final weights are used.

When the neural answer is used (rule v3)

Chosen on tuning data (hand-written tuning labels, held-out titles, held-out place names): Fast keeps the label when it is a place, an address, or already English, or when Fast knew every word. Otherwise the neural answer is used if its confidence is at least 0.1. The English check on the neural answer was dropped: it rejected good answers full of brand names. A label longer than 8 words is treated as an address only if Fast knew most of its words; long product names with a number and a place word were being mistaken for addresses. Tuning score: labels 80.4, titles 65.0 chrF. These settings ship inside the model file, so Python and JavaScript apply the same rule.

v7: one expert per language (mixture of experts)

v6 stays frozen as the shared base. Each of the 16 languages gets its own small expert: an extra pair of matrices (512 → 64 → 512) added to the output of all 12 layers, 786k parameters or 3.15 MB per language. The language detector of the Fast model picks the expert, so only the needed language's parameters are loaded and active; when the detector is unsure, no expert is used and the output is exactly v6. Experts were trained for 4 hours on the labels the detector routes to them, which keeps routing identical in training and on the phone.

Two things limit it. The experts learn from the same data v6 already fitted, so they add capacity but no new knowledge; and the detector is often wrong on short Latin-script labels (it picks the true language for about 87% of the test labels and 76% of real names). The design pays off more when each language has its own extra data or a better teacher.

Hy-MT2-7B takes about 6 seconds per product name on this machine, so it ran on a balanced sample of 330 real names (30 per language). On that same sample: Hy-MT2-7B 57.5, Hy-MT2-1.8B 52.6, NLLB 49.2, OPUS-MT 47.1, Fast 40.2, v6 66.3 chrF.

Why v6 beats even the 7B model on real names. The reference answers are Amazon's own English titles, which are not literal translations: they follow a catalogue style (brand first, a fixed order of attributes, US units). v6 was fine-tuned on 35k such pairs and learned that style; the general translators translate faithfully and lose points for wording. On the short hand-written labels, where a plain correct translation is what counts, Hy-MT2-7B is still clearly ahead (95 vs 80).

On short labels Hy-MT2-7B is the best translator, but at 4.6 GB and seconds per label it can't run on a phone. v6 is about 40× smaller, scores above the OPUS-MT models that wrote most of its training data and level with Hy-MT2-1.8B, and started from a base model that scored 34. The earlier from-scratch models (13–22 MB) stayed near 50.

3. What we did, in order

  • v1–v2 (Nov 4–5). Fast: place-name gazetteer (GeoNames), transliteration for 9 scripts, a product word list, an English check. Runs in ~2 ms per label, in Python and in the browser with identical output.
  • v3 (Nov 5–6). First neural model (7M parameters, NLLB teacher, 100 min). Weight study: removing one decoder attention head helped, so it was kept.
  • v4 (Nov 6–8). Three models: A (NLLB), B (OPUS-MT + M2M100, commercial use OK), C (both). 81k product titles, back-translation into 16 languages, restart-safe training, checkpoint averaging, weight edits per model.
  • v5 (Nov 8–10). Teachers' own English answers added as training targets (knowledge distillation, filtered), Tencent Hy-MT2 added as a third teacher, models B2, C2, H, ALL3, HY. Then MAX: no size limit on data, 35k real multilingual product names (ABO), 60k more titles, 10 h training.
  • Products and word combinations (Nov 10). 952 new product words and 2–3 word phrases mined from the Amazon ESCI and ABO catalogues, translated and round-trip checked, added to every model's word list (1,841 automatic terms in total).
  • New decision rule (Nov 10). When to trust the neural model instead of Fast, tuned on held-out training data. Biggest single gain of v5.
  • v6 (Nov 11–12). Review of the whole project, then the final model: a pretrained compact translator fine-tuned on all our data, new short-label data, a SentencePiece tokenizer written for the phone runtime, better 8-bit conversion, a new decision rule.
  • JEPA (Nov 10–11). A self-supervised encoder pretrained to predict the meaning of a label from another language, used two ways, compared against a baseline trained the same total time.

4. Changing the weights after training

After each model finished, I tested switching off each attention head and each feed-forward block one at a time on one held-out slice, kept a change only if it also helped on a second, separate slice, then tried a 15-minute retraining with the change in place. The final call needs the held-out score up and the hand-written labels not down. Checkpoint averaging (mean of the last 3 saved weights) was tested on every model too.

Old rule: use the neural model only for the words Fast could not translate. New rule: if Fast understood the label (at least one known word) or it looks like an address, keep Fast; otherwise let the neural model translate the whole label, and keep its answer if it is English and keeps the words Fast was sure of. Tuned on the held-out training split, not on the test sets.

6. JEPA

What it is. A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture learns by predicting the meaning vector of hidden or translated text instead of the text itself. Here: an encoder reads a label in one language; a predictor must output the vector that a slowly-updated copy of the encoder (EMA target) gives for the English name, and for masked-out spans of the label. No characters are generated, so it learns meaning, not spelling.

Making it work. The first run collapsed: every label got the same vector. Fixed with centering of the target vectors and a variance/covariance penalty (VICReg). Measured by retrieval: among 1,000 held-out pairs, how often the English name nearest to a foreign label's vector is the right one (chance = 0.1%).

Tuned on held-out data, the best threshold was to never use it: every setting that let it override Fast lowered the score. The nearest known name is usually a related product, not the same one; label wording varies too much for whole-name lookup.

7. What didn't work

  • Word pieces (BPE) instead of characters. Shorter sequences, but on 4 CPU cores the models stayed stuck repeating common words after hours. Characters learn much faster at this size.
  • A 52M-parameter MAX. About 3× slower per step; in the time available it would have seen too little data. The 22M design wins on CPU.
  • Picking one teacher per language. The held-out data was written by OPUS-MT and NLLB, which biases the choice toward them. Using every teacher's filtered answers works better.
  • Hy-MT2 as main data source (HY). Best teacher, but at ~2.5 s per title only 8k pairs were affordable, and its answers in the mixed models (H, ALL3) did not beat OPUS-MT alone.
  • Large weight cuts and long post-edit retraining. See section 4.
  • Training small models from nothing. The biggest lesson of the review: ten hours of from-scratch CPU training reached 38 on held-out titles; fine-tuning a pretrained model passed that in under 15 minutes and ended at 64.
  • Default 8-bit conversion and 7-bit weights for v6. Both lost too much accuracy; see the v6 section.

9. Recommendation

  • Use v11 for real work: the most accurate model, about twice as fast as v6/v7 at half the size, and it marks each answer reliable or needs review. To teach it more, use the post-training tool (see the section on adding new things). Use v8 Fast for quick validation runs. The earlier models are kept for reference only.
  • Earlier advice, still true for v6/v7 (106 MB): it more than doubles the exact-match rate on labels and adds over 20 chrF on real names, and its teachers and data allow commercial use (with attribution).
  • Ship v5 MAX (22 MB) when download size or speed matters more: about 10 ms per short label.
  • Fast alone stays the right choice for addresses and place names (~2 ms); every Accurate model keeps using it for those.
  • Next gains: (1) Hy-MT2-7B scores 95 on the labels; v11 used about 10,000 of its answers, and many more (which needs a GPU) should close much of the remaining gap; (2) Korean is still the weakest language (its word pieces were new to the pretrained model); (3) a smaller v6 by sharing the output layer with the embeddings (about 20 MB less).

10. Conclusions

  1. Two final models. v11 (v8 Complete post-trained three times, with beam search, cleaned data and a reliable flag) is the most accurate and, among the strong models, the fastest and smallest; v8 Fast trades some accuracy for much more speed and is meant for validation.
  2. v11 against Hy-MT2-7B, honestly. Overall on short labels the 7B is still ahead (95 against 88 chrF). On the two thirds of labels v11 marks reliable they are level in chrF; on real product names v11 is ahead; and v11 is about 80 times smaller and 200 times faster. No model here is free of errors: the reliable flag tells which answers to check.
  3. The big jump came with v6. The full system goes from 20.0% exact / 42.6 chrF (rules only) to 65.6% / 79.6 on hand-written labels, and from 39.1 to 66.9 chrF on real marketplace names, fully offline.
  4. Starting from a pretrained model mattered more than everything else combined. Seven days of from-scratch models (v3 to v5, twelve of them) moved label chrF from 43 to 51. One 8.5-hour fine-tune of a pretrained translator moved it to 80.
  5. Data that looks like the task beats more data. 35k real product-name pairs lifted real-name scores by 7 points in v5; 58k short labels were a large part of v6's jump on short labels.
  6. The rules (Fast) remain necessary. Place names and addresses are where the neural models are weakest (about 25 chrF on held-out place names) and Fast is near-perfect, so every system keeps Fast for those.
  7. Weight edits help a little, never a lot. Switching off one or two attention heads gained 0.2–1 chrF on about half the small models. For v6 the weight change that mattered was the 8-bit conversion method (+3 chrF over the default).
  8. Language experts (v7) work, with a small gain. Only the detected language's 3 MB expert is active; labels improve by about 1 chrF over v6. They would matter more with language-specific extra data.
  9. JEPA worked technically but did not pay off with this much translated data: the same hours spent on ordinary training gave a better model.
  10. Against the base models: v6 beats every base model on real product names, including the 7B one (it learned the catalogue's naming style), and beats all but Hy-MT2-7B on short labels. Hy-MT2-7B scores 95 there; distilling it into v6 on a GPU is the clearest path to further gains.

12. Important to know

  • Licenses. v6, v5 MAX, B2, H, HY and v4 B were trained only with teachers and data that allow commercial use (OPUS-MT CC BY 4.0, Hy-MT2 Apache-2.0, ABO CC BY 4.0, ESCI Apache-2.0); attribution is required. v3, v4 A, v4 C, C2 and ALL3 used NLLB (CC BY-NC) and are non-commercial. City names need GeoNames attribution. Whether a teacher's license carries over to a model trained on its outputs is not settled law; get legal advice before selling.
  • Speed and size of v6. 106 MB download; 15–75 ms per label on one server core; about 0.1–0.5 s per label in a single-thread browser (measured in Node, not on a real phone). The small models take about 10–65 ms.
  • It still makes mistakes. About one label in three is not an exact match, and some are plainly wrong (an Arabic "non-stick frying pan" came out as "unsticked mattress"). Korean is the weakest language. For anything where a wrong name is costly, show the original next to the translation.
  • Test honesty. The test files were never trained on, but 60 of 320 labels and 103 of 993 real names also occur by coincidence in training text. The "unseen" columns in the v6 section exclude them and are the strict numbers. The 48 unseen address cases were looked at once while choosing a cautious setting for place names; the score did not change.
  • Python and JavaScript. Identical for Fast, the English check and the tokenizer. For v6 they differ on about 1–3% of labels because 8-bit arithmetic rounds differently; measured quality is the same (79.5 vs 79.6 on labels).
  • GitHub. The pull request is a draft and nothing is merged. GitHub's automatic tests have never run because no runner gets assigned (an Actions setting or billing limit on the account); all tests pass when run here.
  • What is not in the repository. The raw training data (several GB, regenerable with the build scripts), full-precision weights and training checkpoints. They exist only in this session's container, which is temporary. Say so if you want them kept.
  • Privacy. All models run on the device; no label text is sent anywhere.

13. Step by step: how each model was made

Every model, in the order it was made: what the goal was, the steps taken, and what came out. Scores are for the full system on the 320 hand-written labels (chrF) unless stated.

Step 0. English check (Nov 4)

  1. Goal: say in microseconds whether a label is already English, so it can be left alone.
  2. Built a character n-gram scorer from public multilingual text; 100 KB of data.
  3. Wrote it twice (Python and JavaScript) and checked both give identical answers on 12,072 inputs.
  4. Result: about 11 µs per call. Used by every later model.

Step 1. Fast, the rules-only model (v1–v2, Nov 4–5)

  1. Goal: translate places, addresses and common product words with no neural network.
  2. Place names: built a gazetteer from GeoNames and Unicode CLDR (cities, countries, their names in every script), split per script so a phone loads only what it needs.
  3. Transliteration: wrote rules for 9 writing systems (Cyrillic, Arabic, Greek, Devanagari, Thai, Hangul, kana, Han with pinyin, and a fallback).
  4. Language detection: a small n-gram detector for Latin and Han text; other scripts identify the language directly.
  5. Product word list: started with 91 hand-written terms in 16 languages; address rules for street, district, number patterns.
  6. Wrote 70 tuned test cases, then 48 "blind" cases after tuning. Python and JavaScript runtimes checked identical on 3,018 inputs.
  7. Later improvements: replaced a GPL Japanese dictionary with an Apache-licensed one (v4); grew the word list automatically to about 890 terms (v4) and 1,841 (v5) by mining catalogue titles and keeping only translations that survive a round trip.
  8. Result: 42.6 chrF on labels, 70/70 tuned, 44/48 blind, about 2 ms per label.

Step 2. v3, the first neural model (Nov 5–6)

  1. Goal: translate what Fast does not know, in a model small enough for a phone (7 MB).
  2. Data: 2.7k English product titles, translated into the 16 languages by NLLB-600M (the "teacher"), filtered by script, English check and confidence: 24k pairs.
  3. Model: a 7M-parameter character-level encoder-decoder, trained from nothing for 100 minutes on CPU.
  4. Exported to ONNX, converted to 8-bit, wired into "Accurate": Fast first, the neural model only for unknown words, its answer accepted only if it keeps Fast's known words.
  5. Weight study: switched off each attention head in turn; removing one decoder head helped on two separate held-out slices, so it was kept.
  6. Result: 43.2. Lesson: too little data and training; the teacher (NLLB) is non-commercial.

Step 3. v4 A, B and C (Nov 6–8)

  1. Goal: more data, longer training, and a version whose teachers allow commercial use.
  2. Data: 81k English titles from Amazon ESCI. A = translated by NLLB; B = by OPUS-MT (plus M2M100 for Korean and Thai), keeping only translations that translate back to the title; C = both.
  3. Made training restart-safe (the cloud machine restarts often): a checkpoint every 5 minutes and a learning-rate schedule that continues across restarts.
  4. Trained 13M-parameter character models for 4–5 hours each; added a cached decoder (35% faster).
  5. After training: averaged the last 3 checkpoints; tested switching off every head and block; kept a change only if a second held-out slice and the hand-written labels agreed. A stayed as trained, B lost 2 heads, C lost 1.
  6. Results: A 49.6, B 46.5, C 51.0. Lesson: NLLB data teaches better than OPUS-MT data at this size; both together are best.

Step 4. v5 B2, C2, H, ALL3 and HY (Nov 8–10)

  1. Goal: learn more from the teachers and add a third, stronger teacher (Tencent Hy-MT2).
  2. Scored the teachers themselves on the labels: Hy-MT2-7B 95.1, Hy-MT2-1.8B 79.1, OPUS-MT 76.2, NLLB 70.9.
  3. Knowledge distillation: besides the real English title, each teacher's own English answer became an extra training target when it was English and close to the title.
  4. Tried word pieces (BPE) instead of characters, and a 52M-parameter model: both learned too slowly on 4 CPU cores and were dropped. Settled on 22M-parameter character models.
  5. Trained B2 (OPUS-MT), C2 (OPUS-MT + NLLB), H (OPUS-MT + Hy-MT2 answers), ALL3 (all three), each 5 hours; HY (Hy-MT2 writes everything, only 8k pairs because it is slow), 3 hours.
  6. Same weight tests as v4 on each model.
  7. Results: B2 49.8, C2 51.0, H 48.8, ALL3 49.2, HY 45.5. Lesson: teacher answers helped the OPUS-only model (+3 over v4 B); Hy-MT2 answers did not add more; a great teacher with little data loses to an average teacher with a lot.

Step 5. v5 MAX and the new decision rule (Nov 10)

  1. Goal: one model with no limit on data, from commercial-friendly sources only.
  2. Found real paired data: Amazon Berkeley Objects, 55.8k product names written by people in 12 languages with an English name. Held out 993 as a second test set; kept 35k clean pairs for training.
  3. Added 60k more English titles from the same catalogue, translated by OPUS-MT.
  4. Trained for 10 hours on OPUS-MT + Hy-MT2 answers + the real names; switched off 2 heads and retrained 15 minutes.
  5. Decision rule v2: instead of using the neural model only for unknown words, let it translate the whole label whenever Fast did not understand it and it is not an address. Tuned on held-out data.
  6. Result: 49.1 on labels and 45.7 on real names (others about 38.5). Lesson: real data that looks like the task is worth more than anything else so far.

Step 6. JEPA-Gen, JEPA-Lookup and their baseline (Nov 10–11)

  1. Goal: test a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture, which learns by predicting the meaning vector of a label instead of its text.
  2. Built it: an encoder, a slowly-updated copy as target, and a predictor, trained to match a label with its English name and to fill in hidden spans.
  3. First run collapsed (every label got the same vector); fixed with centering and a variance penalty. Tuned 4 settings for 30 minutes each, then a 3-hour main run: the right English name out of 1,000 was found 19% of the time (chance 0.1%).
  4. JEPA-Gen: a translator whose encoder starts from the JEPA encoder, 3 more hours of training.
  5. Baseline: the same model and data with 6 hours of ordinary training, so both had equal total time.
  6. JEPA-Lookup: return the nearest known English name by vector similarity.
  7. Results: JEPA-Gen 43.5, Baseline 45.9; lookup never beat Fast. Lesson: with 400k translated pairs, ordinary training uses the time better.

Step 7. v6, the pretrained translator (Nov 11–12)

  1. Review of everything showed the weak point: the neural part, trained from nothing, scored about 32 alone while big translators score 76–95.
  2. Took a compact pretrained translator, OPUS-MT mul-en (77M parameters, CC BY 4.0). Untrained by us it scored only 34 on labels.
  3. Cut its vocabulary from 64k to 39k pieces (only what our languages use) and added 3,091 pieces it lacked, mostly Korean.
  4. Pilot: 30 minutes of fine-tuning on the MAX data already beat every earlier model.
  5. Noticed the test labels are short but the training text is long titles; made 58k short-label pairs from Amazon search queries translated by OPUS-MT (queries equal to a test answer left out).
  6. Fine-tuned 8.5 hours. Found and fixed a bug that silently dropped training to one CPU thread.
  7. Wrote the model's tokenizer (SentencePiece) in pure Python and JavaScript; it matches the original library on 60,000 of 60,000 strings.
  8. 8-bit conversion: the default lost 5 chrF on short labels; converting each output channel separately got most of it back at the same size.
  9. Decision rule v3, tuned on 192 new hand-written tuning labels: places, addresses and English stay with Fast; long product names with a number are no longer mistaken for addresses; the English check on the answer was dropped.
  10. Result: 79.6 on labels, 66.9 on real names, 106 MB. Lesson: starting from a pretrained model mattered more than everything before it combined.

Step 8. v7, one expert per language (Nov 12)

  1. Goal: a mixture of experts where only the needed language's parameters are active.
  2. Froze v6. Added to each of its 12 layers a small extra pair of matrices per language (3 MB per language), starting as a no-op.
  3. Routing by the Fast model's language detector; experts were trained on what the detector sends them, so training and phone route identically. Unsure labels use plain v6.
  4. Trained the experts 4 hours; exported with the expert as an input to the model, one small file per language loaded on first use.
  5. Result: 80.8 on labels (+1.2 over v6), 67.2 on real names. Lesson: experts trained on the same data add little; they need language-specific data.

Step 9. v8 Complete and v8 Fast, the two final models (Nov 12–13)

  1. Goal: the best accuracy and the best speed together, using everything learned; plus a much faster small model for quick validation.
  2. Measured where v7 was weakest: Korean (55 chrF) and Thai (58), the two languages with no short-label training data, because OPUS-MT cannot write them.
  3. New data: Hy-MT2-1.8B wrote 13k short labels (5,000 Korean, 4,000 Thai, 1,500 each Vietnamese, Japanese, Chinese) from Amazon search queries; kept 12,992 after script and length checks.
  4. Speed by design: the decoder runs once per output token, the encoder once per label. So v8 keeps v6's 6 encoder layers but only 2 of its 6 decoder layers (tested which two to keep), and its output layer covers only the 13.6k word pieces that occur in English instead of the shared 39k.
  5. Mixture of experts without a speed cost: one expert per language in the encoder only (1.6 MB each), chosen by the language detector, trained together with the rest.
  6. Fine-tuned everything for 7 hours on 470k pairs. Right after the cut the model scored 2; after 30 minutes 71; at the end it passed v7.
  7. Exported with per-channel 8-bit weights; retuned the decision rule on the tuning labels; ran the full tests.
  8. v8 Fast: cut again from v8 Complete (3 encoder layers, 1 decoder layer, no experts), fine-tuned 3 hours, same export and tests.
  9. Results: v8 Complete 83.1 on labels and 67.7 on real names; v8 Fast 79.7 and 62.2. Lesson: a shallow decoder plus data aimed at the weakest languages gave more accuracy and more speed at once.

Step 10. v9 and the post-training tool (Nov 13–14)

  1. Goal: get closer to (or past) Hy-MT2 without its size, and make later additions a routine job.
  2. Built three tools: add words instantly (add_terms.py), post-train on new pairs with an automatic pass/fail gate (post_train.sh), and turn English-only lists into pairs (make_pairs.py).
  3. New data: until now each English search query had been translated into only 3 of 14 languages. Translated all 54k queries into 7 languages each with OPUS-MT (393k translations, 251k kept after the round-trip check), plus 6k more Korean and Thai labels from Hy-MT2-1.8B: 199,525 new pairs.
  4. Ran the post-training tool on v8 Complete for 5 hours: new pairs sampled more often, all old data replayed.
  5. The tool exported, retuned, tested parent against child and gave its verdict; then the usual full tests and speed measurements.
  6. Result: v9 86.0 on labels and 67.2 on real names.

Step 11. v10: a diagnosed plan (Nov 14)

  1. Diagnosis on the tuning labels: (a) OPUS-MT had written wrong everyday nouns into the training data (vacuum cleaner as "Vakuum-Reiniger", not "Staubsauger"), so the model never saw the right words; (b) picking only the single best word at each step loses about 2 chrF that a wider search finds; (c) some remaining errors are valid paraphrases the references do not list.
  2. Beam search: re-exported the decoder with a batch axis, wrote the same search in Python and JavaScript, chose the width on tuning data (2 candidates: most of the gain for a few ms).
  3. Head terms: the 1,200 most frequent short search queries, written in all 16 languages by Hy-MT2-1.8B (19,200 translations).
  4. Post-trained v9 with the tool for 3 hours on those pairs (plus the v9 pairs and all old data); the tool chose the beam width, retuned the rule and ran the pass/fail comparison.
  5. Result: v10 86.8 on labels and 67.3 on real names.

Step 12. v11: the 7B model as judge (Nov 14)

  1. Goal: fewer wrong answers at the same speed, and a way to know which answers to trust.
  2. Ran v10 over 28,493 short training labels and kept the 10,685 it missed. Hy-MT2-7B re-translated 8,331 of them (5 hours on CPU, at most 1,500 per language).
  3. Sorted them by the 7B's verdict: old answer wrong (removed), better third answer (replaced), model really wrong (taught again). 5,976 bad pairs left the training data; 5,921 corrected ones came in.
  4. Added 1,558 real Spanish and Japanese queries typed by people, translated by the 7B.
  5. Mixed-language labels: first attempt (two labels joined) made word order worse and was discarded after the tests showed it; second attempt swaps one known word for English inside a real pair (9,600 pairs).
  6. Post-trained v10 for 2.5 hours with the tool. The tool's tuner picked a lower confidence threshold that broke one unseen address case, so the gate said FAIL; with v10's threshold restored it passed.
  7. English double check: found 9 test labels returned untranslated because the English check called them English; added a second opinion from the neural model, tuned on 2,000 English queries and 8,094 foreign labels outside the tests.
  8. Reliable flag: every answer is marked reliable or needs review; threshold set on tuning labels.
  9. Result: v11 87.6 on labels and 67.6 on real names, same size and speed as v10. Lesson: a large part of the remaining error was in the training data, not the model; and a system-level fix (the English check) was worth as much as the retraining.
